\title{An elastic network model whose parameters are rest lengths}
\author{Julian Echave \\ \small (prompting Claude Opus 5)}
\date{2026-08-26}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
In a standard elastic network model the spring constants are set by the
equilibrium distances of a given structure, which makes the contact map a
property of that structure. This is harmless for a single native state but
awkward for mutation, where the object that changes is the sequence, not the
coordinates. We formulate an ENM in which the rest lengths $l_{ij}$ are the only
parameters and the spring constants follow them, $k_{ij} = k(l_{ij})$. The
contact map then becomes a property of the parameters, contacts form and break
through mutation rather than through relaxation, and the mutant structure is
defined as the minimiser of the mutant potential rather than by a linear
response. We derive the resulting theory for a single mutation from an arbitrary
reference: the exact decomposition of the mutation energy into three terms, two
of which vanish when the reference is the relaxed wild type and neither of which
vanishes in general; and the Hessian, whose transverse term is the sole channel
through which frustration reaches the dynamics. The two vanishing terms are
exactly what a treatment calibrated at the wild type discards.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An elastic network model (ENM) represents a protein as beads joined by harmonic
springs, and reproduces the slow collective motions of the native state
remarkably well for so crude a description. In the standard construction one is
handed an experimental structure $\req{}$, declares two residues in contact when
their separation falls below a cutoff, and assigns each contact a spring whose
rest length is the distance observed in that structure. The equilibrium
conformation is then the input structure by construction, and the model is
strain-free: every spring sits at its rest length simultaneously.

This construction ties the model to a structure. That is exactly right when the
question is the dynamics of one native state, and it becomes awkward as soon as
the question is mutation. A mutation changes the sequence; the structure is a
\emph{consequence}. But in the standard formulation the contact map --- which
springs exist at all --- is read off the coordinates, so there is no way to state
what a mutation does to the model without first knowing what it does to the
structure. The usual resort is to hold the contact map fixed and perturb within
it, which forbids contacts from forming or breaking, or to rebuild the model from
a new structure, which presupposes the answer.

There is a second difficulty, less often noticed. Once mutations accumulate, the
reference state is no longer the relaxed wild type. It is a structure that has
been pushed away from every spring's preference and settled at a compromise: a
\emph{frustrated} state, in which no conformation relaxes all springs at once and
the residual strain is nonzero at the minimum. Expressions derived at the wild
type routinely assume a relaxed reference --- often silently, through a term that
happens to vanish there. Applied to a strained reference they are no longer
exact, and the error is not a small correction of fixed sign but a term
proportional to the accumulated strain.

This note develops an ENM designed around both points. The move is small: make
the rest lengths the parameters, and let the spring constants be a function of
the rest lengths,
\begin{equation}
k_{ij} = k(l_{ij}) \qquad \text{rather than} \qquad k_{ij} = k(d^{e}_{ij}),
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
where $d^{e}_{ij}$ is the distance in the equilibrium structure. At the wild type
the two agree, because the standard fit sets $l_{ij} = d^{e}_{ij}$; they part
company for a mutant, and the consequence is the point of the model:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{the contact map becomes a property of the parameters, not of the
structure.}
\end{quote}

Contacts are made and broken by \emph{mutation}, when a rest length crosses the
cutoff --- not by relaxation. A mutation is then a well-defined operation on the
model alone, and the mutant structure follows from it rather than being required
in advance.

We treat a single mutation from an arbitrary reference, which may itself be
strained. Section~\ref{sec:model} sets out the potential and the state,
Section~\ref{sec:mutation} defines a mutation and the mutant structure,
Section~\ref{sec:energy} derives the energy differences and the exact
decomposition that is the main formal result, and Section~\ref{sec:hessian}
derives the Hessian and isolates the transverse term. Throughout, $k(l)$ is an
arbitrary non-negative function; the hard cutoff of the anisotropic network model
(ANM) appears in Section~\ref{sec:step} as a special case, and is the one choice
that makes the energy discontinuous in the parameters.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The potential}

The potential energy of a conformation $\rr$ under parameters $l$ is
\begin{equation}
V(\rr; l) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l_{ij}\big)^{2}.
\label{eq:V}
\end{equation}
Each pair contributes a harmonic term penalising departure of its current
distance from its rest length, with a stiffness fixed by that same rest length.
We take the zero of energy at the fully relaxed configuration, so that $V$
evaluated at any conformation is exactly the strain energy stored in the
springs.

The sum runs over all $\tfrac12 N(N-1)$ distinct pairs. Pairs with $k(l_{ij}) =
0$ contribute nothing and are \emph{inactive}; those with $k(l_{ij}) > 0$ are
\emph{active}, and the set of active pairs is the contact map. Because $k$ is
evaluated at $l_{ij}$ and not at $d_{ij}(\rr)$, membership of the contact map is
determined by the parameters alone, independently of the conformation at which
$V$ is being evaluated. This is the defining property of the model and every
result below traces back to it.

\subsection{States}

A state of the model is a complete assignment of rest lengths, one for every pair
of sites --- not merely for the active ones. The wild-type state is obtained from
an experimental structure $\req{\text{wt}}$ by
\begin{equation}
l^{\text{wt}}_{ij} \;=\; d_{ij}(\req{\text{wt}}),
\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
which is the standard ENM fit, and reproduces the standard model exactly: every
spring sits at its rest length at $\req{\text{wt}}$, so the wild-type structure is
a minimum of $V$ with zero strain energy, and $k(l^{\text{wt}}_{ij}) =
k(d^{e}_{ij})$ recovers the usual assignment.

Carrying rest lengths for inactive pairs is forced by the requirement that
contacts be able to form. By eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} every active wild-type pair has a
rest length inside the cutoff; if inactive pairs carried no parameter, no pair
outside the cutoff could ever be brought inside one, and mutation could only
remove contacts. Assigning every pair a rest length makes formation and breaking
symmetric operations. The enlargement is one of parameters only: inactive pairs
have $k = 0$ and contribute neither energy nor curvature, so the Hessian retains
its $3N \times 3N$ form and the active pairs alone determine the dynamics.

\subsection{Strain and frustration}

For a state $l$ with equilibrium structure $\req{}$, define the residual of a
pair as the amount by which its spring is stretched or compressed at equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
r_{ij} \;\equiv\; d_{ij}(\req{}) - l_{ij},
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
and the strain energy as $E_{\text{strain}} = V(\req{}; l)$, which by
eq.~\eqref{eq:V} is $\tfrac12\sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij}) r_{ij}^2$.

A state is \textbf{frustrated} when no conformation relaxes every active spring
at once, equivalently when $E_{\text{strain}} > 0$ at the minimum. The wild-type
state is unfrustrated by construction: eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} makes every residual
vanish at $\req{\text{wt}}$. A mutant is frustrated in general, because there is
no reason a perturbed set of rest lengths should be simultaneously realisable by
any embedding of $N$ points in three dimensions. Frustration is thus the generic
condition of the model and the unfrustrated wild type is the special case ---
the reverse of the usual situation, and the reason the terms identified in
Section~\ref{sec:energy} cannot be dismissed.

\section{Mutation}
\label{sec:mutation}

A mutation at site $s$ perturbs the rest lengths of all pairs involving that
site,
\begin{equation}
l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} \;=\; l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij},
\qquad
\delta l_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \ \text{ for } i = s \text{ or } j = s,
\label{eq:mutation}
\end{equation}
and leaves all other rest lengths unchanged. The parameter $\sigma$ sets the
mutation size. The spring constants are not free: they follow the rest lengths
through eq.~\eqref{eq:kl}, so the mutation induces
\begin{equation}
\delta k_{ij} \;=\; k(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}) - k(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}),
\label{eq:dk}
\end{equation}
which is nonzero precisely for those pairs whose rest length has moved into a
region of different stiffness --- in particular, across the cutoff. Contacts
form and break here, in the parameters, and nowhere else.

The mutant structure is defined as a minimiser of the mutant potential,
\begin{equation}
\req{\text{mut}} \;=\; \arg\min_{\rr} V(\rr; l^{\text{mut}}),
\label{eq:minimiser}
\end{equation}
and the mutant's energy is $V(\req{\text{mut}}; l^{\text{mut}})$, read at that
minimum. Two features of this definition are worth making explicit, because they
are what distinguish the model from the usual perturbative treatments.

First, \textbf{no expansion conditions the structure}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:minimiser}
is a statement about the exact potential, not about a quadratic approximation to
it; the mutant structure is not $\req{\text{ref}}$ displaced by a linear response
$\mathbf{C}_{\text{ref}}\mathbf{f}$, and it is not obtained by truncating
anything. Normal modes, when computed, describe a state that has already been
determined; they never determine it.

Second, \textbf{the contact map may differ between reference and mutant}, by
eq.~\eqref{eq:dk}. The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:energy} is therefore
between two genuinely different Hamiltonians --- not merely between two parameter
values of one Hamiltonian --- and the bookkeeping must accommodate springs that
exist on one side and not the other.

\section{Energy differences}
\label{sec:energy}

\subsection{Three differences}

Comparing a mutant to its reference involves two potentials and two structures,
so a difference in energy is ambiguous until both are named. Three combinations
are meaningful. The \emph{stress} energy is the cost of changing the Hamiltonian
while holding the structure fixed at the reference:
\begin{equation}
\Vstress \;=\; V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{ref}}).
\label{eq:dvstress}
\end{equation}
The \emph{relaxation} energy is the energy released when the mutant Hamiltonian
is allowed to find its own minimum:
\begin{equation}
\Vrelax \;=\; V(\req{\text{mut}}; l^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{mut}}) \;\le\; 0.
\label{eq:dvrelax}
\end{equation}
It is non-positive because $\req{\text{mut}}$ minimises $V(\cdot; l^{\text{mut}})$
by eq.~\eqref{eq:minimiser}. Their sum is the difference of minima,
\begin{equation}
\Vmin \;=\; V(\req{\text{mut}}; l^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{ref}})
\;=\; \Vstress + \Vrelax,
\label{eq:dvmin}
\end{equation}
which compares each state at its own equilibrium and is the quantity a selective
criterion would act on. In words: two Hamiltonians at one structure; one
Hamiltonian at two structures; and the difference of minima.

\subsection{The exact decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The stress energy can be written out exactly. Substituting
$l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}$ and $k^{\text{mut}}_{ij}
= k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta k_{ij}$ into eq.~\eqref{eq:dvstress}, with
$r_{ij}$ the reference's own residual from eq.~\eqref{eq:residual}, gives
\begin{equation}
\Vstress \;=\;
\underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{(a)}}
\;\underbrace{-\;\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, r_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}}_{\text{(b)}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_{i<j} \delta k_{ij}\big(r_{ij} - \delta l_{ij}\big)^{2}}_{\text{(c)}}.
\label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
This is an identity, not an expansion: no term has been truncated, and it holds
for arbitrary $k(l)$, arbitrary mutation size, and an arbitrarily strained
reference. The three terms have distinct characters.

\paragraph{(a) The direct cost.} Always present and always positive, this is the
work done stretching the springs that already exist, and it is the only term that
survives when the reference is relaxed and the contact map is unchanged. It is
quadratic in the mutation size and independent of the reference's strain.

\paragraph{(b) The cross term.} This term couples the mutation to the strain the
reference already carries. It vanishes if and only if the reference is relaxed,
$r_{ij} = 0$ --- in particular it vanishes at the wild type, by
eq.~\eqref{eq:fit}. It is the only term of the three that can be negative: a
mutation that happens to move rest lengths toward the distances the structure
already exhibits releases energy. Its magnitude is proportional to the reference's
accumulated strain, so it is negligible near the wild type and grows as the
reference becomes more frustrated. A formula calibrated at the wild type
therefore appears correct, and degrades in exactly the regime where mutations
have accumulated.

\paragraph{(c) The contact term.} This term exists only because $k$ follows $l$.
It vanishes if and only if $\delta k_{ij} = 0$ for all pairs, which is the
assumption made by models that perturb within a fixed contact map --- the
linearly forced ENM (LFENM) among them. Here a pair whose stiffness changes
contributes its whole stored energy, and if $k(l)$ is a step then that
contribution arrives discontinuously (Section~\ref{sec:step}). Note that (c) is
governed by $r_{ij} - \delta l_{ij}$, the residual the pair \emph{would} have
under the mutant rest length, so a contact forming far from its new rest length
is expensive.

Terms (b) and (c) are precisely what is discarded by a treatment calibrated at
the relaxed wild type with a frozen contact map. Neither is a higher-order
correction that could be recovered by expanding further; both are exact
contributions that happen to vanish in that special case.

\subsection{Differences, not energies}

One consequence deserves emphasis because it is a standing source of error.
$\Vstress$ is a \emph{difference}, and it is not interchangeable with the
absolute energy $V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{mut}})$ of the mutant Hamiltonian at
the reference structure. The two differ by $V(\req{\text{ref}}; l^{\text{ref}})$,
the reference's own strain energy, which is zero at the wild type and grows as
the reference becomes frustrated. At the wild type the distinction is invisible;
away from it, it is the whole of the reference's accumulated strain.

\section{Dynamics}
\label{sec:hessian}

\subsection{The Hessian}

The motions of a state are governed by the second derivatives of $V$ at its
equilibrium structure. Differentiating eq.~\eqref{eq:V} twice with respect to the
coordinates gives, for the off-diagonal $3\times3$ block coupling two active
sites $i$ and $j$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{ij} \;=\; -k(l_{ij})\Big[
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}}
+ g_{ij}\big(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}} - \mathbf{I}\big)\Big],
\qquad
g_{ij} \;=\; \frac{l_{ij}}{d_{ij}(\rr)} - 1,
\label{eq:hessian}
\end{equation}
with the diagonal blocks fixed by translational invariance,
$\mathbf{K}_{ii} = -\sum_{j \ne i}\mathbf{K}_{ij}$. Inactive pairs have
$k(l_{ij}) = 0$ and drop out, so the matrix is $3N \times 3N$ regardless of how
many pairs carry parameters. It has six zero eigenvalues, corresponding to the
three translations and three rotations under which $V$ is invariant.

\subsection{The transverse term}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian} contains two pieces. The first,
$\hat{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\mathsf{T}}$, is the longitudinal projector:
it resists stretching along the line joining the two sites and is the whole of
the standard ENM Hessian. The second, weighted by $g_{ij}$, acts in the plane
\emph{perpendicular} to that line, and is the \textbf{transverse term}. Its
coefficient
\begin{equation}
g_{ij} \;=\; \frac{l_{ij}}{d_{ij}(\rr)} - 1
\end{equation}
measures the fractional mismatch between the spring's rest length and its actual
extension. A stretched spring ($d_{ij} > l_{ij}$) has $g_{ij} < 0$ and resists
transverse displacement, like a taut string; a compressed one has $g_{ij} > 0$
and assists it.

The transverse term vanishes identically when every spring sits at its rest
length. This gives the model's sharpest structural statement:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{frustration reaches the dynamics only through $g_{ij}$.}
\end{quote}

A state's residuals enter the energy through the strain $E_{\text{strain}}$ and
enter the motions through $g_{ij}$, and through nothing else. Standard ENMs omit
the transverse term, and are entitled to: their fit, eq.~\eqref{eq:fit}, forces
$l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\req{})$, hence $g_{ij} = 0$ at the structure where the Hessian
is evaluated. The omission is exact for an unfrustrated state and is an
approximation of uncontrolled sign for any other --- including every mutant of
the present model.

It is worth distinguishing the transverse term from the cross term (b) of
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}, since both vanish at a relaxed reference and
both are proportional to residuals. They are different objects: (b) is a
contribution to an energy difference, linear in the mutation; $g_{ij}$ is a
contribution to the curvature at a single state, independent of any mutation.
A state can have no mutation applied to it and still have $g_{ij} \ne 0$.

\section{The step-function case}
\label{sec:step}

The theory above holds for arbitrary $k(l)$. The ANM corresponds to the hard
cutoff
\begin{equation}
k(l) \;=\; k_0\,\mathds{1}[\,l \le d_{\max}\,],
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
in which a pair is either a full-strength spring or absent. This choice is the
common one and it has a specific consequence: $V$ is \emph{discontinuous} in the
parameters. A rest length crossing $d_{\max}$ adds or removes a spring outright,
and by term (c) of eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} the energy jumps by
$\tfrac12 k_0 (r_{ij} - \delta l_{ij})^2$, an amount that does not go to zero as
the crossing becomes marginal. Two states with almost identical parameters can
have appreciably different energies.

Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:hessian} requires this. A smooth
$k(l)$ --- a sigmoid of width $w$ centred on $d_{\max}$, say --- makes $V$
continuous and differentiable in the parameters, makes $\delta k_{ij}$ small
whenever $\delta l_{ij}$ is small, and reduces term (c) to a smooth
contribution rather than a jump. The price is that the contact map ceases to be a
crisp binary object: every pair carries some stiffness, and "the contact map"
becomes a thresholded description of a continuum rather than a property of the
state. The step and the sigmoid are thus two ends of a modelling choice about
how sharply stiffness should fall with rest length, and the theory is written to
accommodate either.

\section{Discussion}

The model is a small departure from the standard ENM --- one argument changed,
from $k(d^{e}_{ij})$ to $k(l_{ij})$ --- and the consequences follow from that
single change. Making the rest lengths the parameters makes the contact map a
property of the parameters; that in turn makes mutation a well-defined operation
on the model that can create and destroy contacts, and makes the mutant structure
a derived quantity rather than an input.

The formal content is eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} and eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian}.
Both exhibit the same pattern: a term that is present in general, plus terms that
vanish precisely when the reference is the relaxed wild type with a fixed contact
map. In the energy these are the cross term (b), proportional to accumulated
strain, and the contact term (c), proportional to changes in stiffness. In the
dynamics it is the transverse term $g_{ij}$. A treatment calibrated at the wild
type sets all three to zero and is exactly right in doing so --- at the wild
type. The model's purpose is to describe states reached by mutation, where none
of the three is zero, and where the discarded terms grow with the very quantity
that accumulates.

Two limitations of scope should be stated. We have treated a single mutation from
a given reference; the reference may be arbitrarily strained, so the results
apply at any point of a substitution process, but the process itself --- how
mutations are proposed, accepted, and accumulated --- is not developed here, and
neither are the statistics of strain along such a process. And the theory is
formal throughout: it establishes what the terms are and when they vanish, not
how large they are in any particular protein, which is an empirical question the
decomposition is designed to make answerable.
