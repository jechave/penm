\title{A non-perturbative elastic network model of mutation}
\author{Julian Echave \\ \small (prompting Claude Opus 5)}
\date{2026-08-26}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
An elastic network model is specified by two sets of parameters, the spring
constants $k_{ij}$ and the equilibrium lengths $l_{ij}$. Standard practice fixes
both from a single experimental structure, setting $l_{ij} = d^{e}_{ij}$ and
$k_{ij} = f(d^{e}_{ij})$, so that the potential is a function of that structure.
Representing a mutation is then not straightforward. Perturbing the spring
constants alone changes the dynamics but leaves the equilibrium structure
unchanged, so a structural response requires perturbing the equilibrium lengths.
This is what the linearly forced ENM (LFENM) does, treating the consequences
perturbatively. Here we ask how far the same picture can be carried with no
linearisation, no expansion, and no fixed contact map. We take the equilibrium
lengths as the parameters, couple the spring constants to them through $k_{ij} =
k(l_{ij})$, and define the mutant structure as the conformation that minimises
the mutant potential. We derive two results for a single mutation from an
arbitrary reference, which may itself be strained: a decomposition of the energy
cost of a mutation into three terms, and the Hessian of a strained network, in
which a transverse contribution absent from standard ENMs carries the effect of
frustration on the dynamics. In both cases the extra terms are those that vanish
for an unstrained reference with a fixed contact map.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An elastic network model (ENM) represents a protein as beads joined by harmonic
springs. Each spring is specified by two parameters, a spring constant $k_{ij}$
and an equilibrium length $l_{ij}$, so the potential energy is
\begin{equation}
V(\rr) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k_{ij}\,\big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l_{ij}\big)^{2},
\label{eq:generic}
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij}(\rr)$ is the distance between beads $i$ and $j$ in conformation
$\rr$. Despite its simplicity the model reproduces the slow collective motions of
native proteins well.

Both parameter sets are usually read from one experimental structure $\req{}$: we
set $l_{ij} = d^{e}_{ij}$ and take $k_{ij} = f(d^{e}_{ij})$ for some decreasing
function $f$, often a step at a cutoff distance. The potential is then a function
of that structure. Two properties follow. The structure is an equilibrium
conformation by construction, and the network is unstrained there, all springs
sitting at their equilibrium lengths at once.

\subsection{Representing a mutation}

How should a mutation be represented in such a model? The two parameter sets play
different roles. Perturbing the spring constants $k_{ij}$ modifies the stiffness
matrix and changes the normal modes, but leaves the equilibrium conformation
where it was: a network whose springs already sit at their equilibrium lengths
stays at equilibrium however the stiffnesses are scaled, so such a perturbation
cannot be used to predict conformational change \cite{echave2010}. For a mutation
to move the structure it must perturb the equilibrium lengths.

This is the picture behind the linearly forced ENM (LFENM)
\cite{echave2008,echave2010}, in which a mutation perturbs the equilibrium
lengths of the springs at the mutated site, $l_{ij} \to l_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}$,
and the structural consequence is obtained perturbatively. Expanding the
resulting change in energy to first order in the displacements recasts the
perturbation as a set of forces directed along the perturbed contacts,
$\mathbf{f}_{ij} = k_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}$, with a reaction
force at the mutated site that makes the total vanish. The mutant potential
becomes harmonic-plus-linear, and the structural change follows in closed form as
a linear response, $\delta \bar{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}$, with
$\mathbf{K}^{-1}$ the pseudo-inverse taken over the $3N-6$ nonzero modes. The
stiffness matrix itself is left unchanged, so the mutant's normal modes are those
of the reference. A self-consistent variant recovers them by taking the mutant
structure predicted this way and rebuilding the network from it, which is how the
effect of mutations on normal modes has been studied \cite{echave2012}.

Two approximations enter here. The ENM potential is expanded to second order in
the displacements, which discards a transverse contribution to the stiffness
matrix and is exact only when every spring sits at its equilibrium length. The
energy change due to the mutation is then expanded to first order, which is what
produces the forces. Both are controlled at an unstrained wild type. Neither is
obviously controlled once mutations have accumulated.

Here we ask how far the same picture can be carried with no linearisation. Every
step below is exact: no expansion of the potential, no linear response, no
truncation, and no fixed contact map. This is not to say the perturbative
treatment is wrong. Its accuracy and range of validity cannot be assessed from
within itself, and an exact formulation is what one compares against.

\subsection{Two assumptions of the standard setup}

Working exactly makes visible two assumptions built into the standard
construction.

The first concerns the contact map. Since $k_{ij}$ is a function of the
equilibrium \emph{distance} $d^{e}_{ij}$, which pairs are connected is a property
of the structure. A mutation that changes equilibrium lengths then has no defined
effect on the set of springs until the new structure is known, and the new
structure cannot be computed without knowing the set of springs. One way out is
to freeze the contact map, which forbids contacts from forming or breaking.
Another is to iterate, predicting a structure with the springs one has and then
rebuilding the springs from that structure. We avoid the circularity instead, by
coupling the spring constant to the equilibrium \emph{length} rather than to the
distance,
\begin{equation}
k_{ij} \;=\; k(l_{ij}),
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
which makes the set of active springs a property of the parameters alone. A
contact then forms or breaks when a mutation moves an equilibrium length across
the range where $k$ falls off, and this happens in the parameters, before any
structure is computed. The parameters determine the structure and not the reverse.

The second concerns strain. Once a mutation has been applied, there is no reason
the perturbed equilibrium lengths should be simultaneously realisable by any
arrangement of beads in space, so the network settles at a compromise in which
some springs remain stretched or compressed. Such a network is frustrated.
Proteins are known to be frustrated, and frustration is thought to matter.
Expressions derived for an unstrained network often contain terms that vanish
there and are, for that reason, easy to omit without comment. Applied to a
strained network they are no longer exact, and the discarded terms grow with the
accumulated strain.

We take the wild type to be unstrained, since that is what the standard fit
$l_{ij} = d^{e}_{ij}$ gives, but nothing below requires it. The theory is written
for an arbitrary reference network, strained or not. This matters for
evolutionary trajectories, where each fixed mutant becomes the reference for the
next mutation and is generally strained.

\subsection{Scope}

We treat a single mutation from an arbitrary reference network. Section
\ref{sec:model} defines the potential, the parameters and the state, and makes
strain and frustration precise. Section \ref{sec:mutation} defines a mutation and
the mutant structure. Section \ref{sec:energy} derives the energy cost of a
mutation and decomposes it exactly into three contributions. Section
\ref{sec:hessian} derives the Hessian of a strained network, which governs its
motions, and identifies the contribution through which frustration acts. Section
\ref{sec:step} examines what changes when $k(l)$ is taken to be a step function.
The substitution process itself, and the statistics of strain along an
evolutionary trajectory, are not treated here.

The subscripts \emph{ref} and \emph{mut} distinguish the reference network from

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The potential}

The potential energy of a conformation $\rr$ for a network with equilibrium
lengths $\LL$ is
\begin{equation}
V(\rr; \LL) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l_{ij}\big)^{2}.
\label{eq:V}
\end{equation}
This is eq.~\eqref{eq:generic} with the spring constants tied to the equilibrium
lengths through eq.~\eqref{eq:kl}, so that $\LL$ alone specifies the network. We
take the zero of energy at the fully relaxed configuration, so $V$ evaluated at
any conformation is the strain energy stored in the springs.

The sum runs over all $\tfrac12 N(N-1)$ pairs. Pairs with $k(l_{ij}) = 0$
contribute nothing and are inactive; those with $k(l_{ij}) > 0$ are active, and
the active pairs are the contact map. Since $k$ is evaluated at $l_{ij}$ rather
than at $d_{ij}(\rr)$, membership of the contact map depends only on the
parameters, whatever conformation $V$ is evaluated at. The results below follow
from this.

The function $k(l)$ is arbitrary and non-negative. We would normally take it
decreasing, so that long springs are weak or absent, and if it falls to zero
beyond some distance then that distance is a cutoff. A cutoff is a feature of a
particular choice of $k$, not of the framework, and nothing below requires one.

\subsection{States}

A state of the model is an assignment of equilibrium lengths to every pair of
sites, not only to the active ones. The wild-type state is obtained from an
experimental structure $\req{\text{wt}}$ in the standard way,
\begin{equation}
l^{\text{wt}}_{ij} \;=\; d_{ij}(\req{\text{wt}}),
\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
which reproduces the usual model: every spring sits at its equilibrium length at
$\req{\text{wt}}$, so that structure is a minimum of $V$ with zero strain energy,
and $k(l^{\text{wt}}_{ij}) = k(d^{e}_{ij})$ recovers the usual assignment of
spring constants.

Carrying equilibrium lengths for inactive pairs is required if contacts are to
form. By eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} every active wild-type pair has an equilibrium length
inside the range where $k$ is nonzero. If inactive pairs carried no parameter,
none could ever be brought into that range, and mutation could only remove
contacts. Giving every pair an equilibrium length makes formation and breaking
symmetric. This costs nothing dynamically, since inactive pairs have $k = 0$ and
contribute neither energy nor curvature: the Hessian keeps its $3N \times 3N$
form and the active pairs alone determine the motions.

\subsection{Strain and frustration}

Let $\req{}$ be an equilibrium conformation, where $V$ attains its minimum, and
define
\begin{equation}
s_{ij} \;\equiv\; l_{ij} - d_{ij}(\req{}),
\label{eq:sij}
\end{equation}
the amount by which the spring on pair $ij$ is compressed ($s_{ij} > 0$) or
stretched ($s_{ij} < 0$) at equilibrium. The strain energy of the network is the
value of the potential there,
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{strain}} \;=\; V(\req{}; \LL) \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\, s_{ij}^{2}.
\label{eq:estrain}
\end{equation}

A network is \textbf{frustrated} when the equilibrium conformation, which relaxes
the network as far as it can be relaxed, still leaves some springs away from
their equilibrium lengths. It follows that $E_{\text{strain}} > 0$.

The wild-type state of eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} is unfrustrated: all $s_{ij}$ vanish at
$\req{\text{wt}}$. A mutant is frustrated in general, since perturbed equilibrium
lengths need not be simultaneously realisable by any arrangement of $N$ points in
three dimensions. Frustration is the generic case here and the unstrained network
the exception, the reverse of the situation the standard construction is built
for.

\section{Mutation}
\label{sec:mutation}

A mutation at site $m$ perturbs the equilibrium lengths of the springs attached
to that site,
\begin{equation}
l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} \;=\; l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij},
\qquad
\delta l_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
\ \text{ for } i = m \text{ or } j = m,
\label{eq:mutation}
\end{equation}
leaving all others unchanged; $\sigma$ sets the size of the mutation. This is the
LFENM's representation of a mutation, and it is adopted here unchanged. What
differs is what follows from it.

The spring constants are not independent parameters: they follow the equilibrium
lengths through eq.~\eqref{eq:kl}, so the mutation induces
\begin{equation}
\delta k_{ij} \;=\; k(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}) - k(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}),
\label{eq:dk}
\end{equation}
nonzero for any pair whose equilibrium length has moved into a region of
different stiffness. Contacts form and break here, in the parameters, and nowhere
else.

The mutant structure is the conformation at which the mutant potential attains
its minimum,
\begin{equation}
\req{\text{mut}} \;:\; V(\req{\text{mut}}; \LL^{\text{mut}})
= \min_{\rr} V(\rr; \LL^{\text{mut}}),
\label{eq:minimum}
\end{equation}
and the mutant's energy is $V$ evaluated there. Two features of this definition
matter.

First, no expansion conditions the structure. Eq.~\eqref{eq:minimum} refers to
the exact potential, not to a quadratic approximation of it. The mutant structure
is not the reference displaced by a linear response to an equivalent force, and
it is not obtained by truncating anything. Normal modes, when we compute them,
describe a structure that has already been determined; they do not determine it.

Second, the contact map may differ between reference and mutant, by
eq.~\eqref{eq:dk}. The comparison in Section \ref{sec:energy} is then between two
networks that need not have the same springs, and the bookkeeping has to
accommodate springs present on one side and absent on the other.

\section{The energy cost of a mutation}
\label{sec:energy}

\subsection{Three differences}

Comparing a mutant with its reference involves two potentials and two
conformations, so an energy difference is ambiguous until both are named. Three
combinations are useful. The first is the cost of changing the network while
holding the conformation fixed at the reference equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\Vstress \;=\; V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{ref}}).
\label{eq:dvstress}
\end{equation}
The second is the energy released when the mutant network relaxes to its own
equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\Vrelax \;=\; V(\req{\text{mut}}; \LL^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{mut}}) \;\le\; 0,
\label{eq:dvrelax}
\end{equation}
non-positive because $\req{\text{mut}}$ is where $V(\cdot\,; \LL^{\text{mut}})$
attains its minimum. Their sum compares each network at its own equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\Vmin \;=\; V(\req{\text{mut}}; \LL^{\text{mut}}) - V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{ref}})
\;=\; \Vstress + \Vrelax.
\label{eq:dvmin}
\end{equation}
The first compares two networks at one conformation, the second one network at
two conformations, and the third is the difference of minima.

\subsection{An exact decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The first of these can be written out in full. Substituting
$l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}$ and
$k^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta k_{ij}$ into
eq.~\eqref{eq:dvstress}, and writing $s_{ij}$ for the reference network's own
departures from equilibrium, eq.~\eqref{eq:sij}, gives
\begin{equation}
\Vstress \;=\;
\underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{(a)}}
\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, s_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}}_{\text{(b)}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_{i<j} \delta k_{ij}\big(s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}\big)^{2}}_{\text{(c)}}.
\label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
This is an identity. Nothing has been truncated, and it holds for any $k(l)$, any
mutation size, and any reference network however strained. The three terms behave
differently.

Term (a) is the work of stretching the springs that already exist. It is
quadratic in the mutation size, always positive, and independent of the
reference's strain. It is the only term that survives for an unstrained reference
with an unchanged contact map.

Term (b) couples the mutation to the strain the reference already carries. It
vanishes if and only if the reference is unstrained, all $s_{ij} = 0$, which is
the case at the wild type by eq.~\eqref{eq:fit}. It is the only term of the three
that can be negative: a mutation that happens to move equilibrium lengths toward
the distances the structure already exhibits releases energy. Its size is
proportional to the reference's strain, so it is negligible near the wild type
and grows as the reference becomes more frustrated.

Term (c) is present only because the spring constants follow the equilibrium
lengths. It vanishes if and only if every $\delta k_{ij} = 0$, which is what a
fixed contact map assumes. Here a pair whose stiffness changes contributes its
whole stored energy. Note that it is governed by $s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}$, the
departure the pair would have under its new equilibrium length, so a contact
forming far from that length is expensive.

Terms (b) and (c) are what is discarded by a treatment set up at an unstrained
reference with a frozen contact map. Neither is a higher-order correction to be
recovered by expanding further. Both are contributions that vanish in that
special case.

\jc{Open question. How large are (b) and (c) for a real protein, relative to (a)
and to each other? The decomposition says when they vanish, not how much they
matter. This is what the numerical work is for.}

\subsection{Differences, not energies}

$\Vstress$ is a difference, and is not interchangeable with the absolute energy
$V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{mut}})$ of the mutant network at the reference
conformation. The two differ by $V(\req{\text{ref}}; \LL^{\text{ref}})$, the
reference's own strain energy, which is zero at the wild type and grows as the
reference becomes frustrated. At the wild type the distinction is invisible. Away
from it, it is the whole of the accumulated strain.

\section{The dynamics of a strained network}
\label{sec:hessian}

\subsection{The Hessian}

Small motions about equilibrium are governed by the second derivatives of $V$,
the Hessian, whose off-diagonal $3\times3$ blocks couple pairs of sites.
Differentiating eq.~\eqref{eq:V} twice with respect to the coordinates gives, for
an active pair,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{ij} \;=\; -k(l_{ij})\Big[
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}}
+ g_{ij}\big(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}} - \mathbf{I}\big)\Big],
\qquad
g_{ij} \;=\; \frac{l_{ij}}{d_{ij}(\rr)} - 1,
\label{eq:hessian}
\end{equation}
with the diagonal blocks fixed by translational invariance,
$\mathbf{K}_{ii} = -\sum_{j \ne i}\mathbf{K}_{ij}$.\footnote{The sign of the
transverse coefficient is easy to get wrong. The form above, with
$g_{ij} = l_{ij}/d_{ij} - 1$, was checked against numerical second differences of
eq.~\eqref{eq:V} at a frustrated minimum, and agrees to eight significant
figures; the opposite sign does not.} Inactive pairs drop out, so the matrix is
$3N \times 3N$ however many pairs carry parameters. It has six zero eigenvalues,
from the three translations and three rotations that leave $V$ unchanged.

\subsection{The transverse contribution}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian} has two parts. The projector
$\hat{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\mathsf{T}}$ resists stretching along the line
joining the two sites, and is the whole of the standard ENM Hessian. The part
weighted by $g_{ij}$ acts in the plane perpendicular to that line. Its
coefficient measures the fractional mismatch between the spring's equilibrium
length and its actual extension, and in terms of the departure from equilibrium
is simply
\begin{equation}
g_{ij} \;=\; \frac{s_{ij}}{d_{ij}(\rr)}.
\label{eq:gs}
\end{equation}
Grouping eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian} by projector makes the two directions explicit.
Writing $\mathbf{P}_\perp = \mathbf{I} - \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}}$
for the projector onto the plane perpendicular to the bond,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{ij} \;=\; -k(l_{ij})\Big[
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}} - g_{ij}\,\mathbf{P}_\perp\Big],
\label{eq:hessian-split}
\end{equation}
so a pair has two effective force constants, not one: a longitudinal $k(l_{ij})$,
independent of $g_{ij}$ and always positive, and a transverse $-k(l_{ij})g_{ij}$,
doubly degenerate, whose sign follows that of $s_{ij}$. For an isolated pair
these are the two nonzero eigenvalues of the $6\times6$ Hessian, equal to
$2k(l_{ij})$ and $-2k(l_{ij})g_{ij}$.

A stretched spring ($s_{ij} < 0$) resists transverse displacement, as a taut
string does. A compressed one ($s_{ij} > 0$) assists it, and its transverse
constant is negative: moving the two beads sideways lengthens the pair, which is
what a compressed spring wants. The spring constant $k(l_{ij})$ itself is never
negative. An isolated compressed pair is therefore unstable, while a compressed
pair inside a network need not be, since the negative transverse curvature is one
contribution among many.

The transverse part vanishes when every spring is at its equilibrium length.
Frustration reaches the dynamics through $g_{ij}$ and through nothing else: the
departures $s_{ij}$ enter the energy through eq.~\eqref{eq:estrain} and the
motions through eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian}, and nowhere else.

Standard ENMs omit the transverse part, and are entitled to, because their fit
forces $l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\req{})$ and hence $g_{ij} = 0$ at the conformation where
the Hessian is evaluated. This is what the second-order expansion of the
potential in the displacements accomplishes: keeping $(d_{ij} - l_{ij})^2
\approx (\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{ij})^2$ retains the longitudinal
projector and drops the rest. The omission is exact for an unstrained network and
an approximation of uncontrolled sign for any other, including every mutant
here.

We should separate this from term (b) of Section \ref{sec:decomposition}, since
both vanish for an unstrained reference and both are proportional to the
$s_{ij}$. They are different objects. Term (b) contributes to an energy
difference and is linear in the mutation; $g_{ij}$ contributes to the curvature
of a single network and has nothing to do with any mutation. A network with no
mutation applied to it can have $g_{ij} \ne 0$.

\jc{Open question. How far can $s_{ij}$ grow before a compressed spring stops
representing anything we mean by a residue contact? Nothing in the formalism
breaks, and a network can carry compressed springs and remain stable, but a
spring with $l_{ij}$ well above $d_{ij}$ is a strut under load rather than a weak
contact. This bears on how large a mutation the model can be asked to describe.}

\section{The step-function case}
\label{sec:step}

Everything above holds for arbitrary $k(l)$. The common choice is a step at a
cutoff $d_{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
k(l) \;=\; k_0\,\mathds{1}[\,l \le d_{\max}\,],
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
so a pair is either a full-strength spring or absent. This makes $V$
discontinuous in the parameters. An equilibrium length crossing $d_{\max}$ adds
or removes a spring outright, and by term (c) of eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} the
energy jumps by $\tfrac12 k_0 (s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij})^2$, which does not go to
zero as the crossing becomes marginal. Two networks with nearly identical
parameters can differ appreciably in energy.

Nothing in the preceding sections requires this. A smooth $k(l)$, a sigmoid of
width $w$ centred on $d_{\max}$ for instance, makes $V$ continuous and
differentiable in the parameters, keeps $\delta k_{ij}$ small whenever $\delta
l_{ij}$ is small, and turns term (c) into a smooth contribution rather than a
jump. The price is that the contact map stops being a binary object: every pair
carries some stiffness, and the contact map becomes a thresholded description of
a continuum. The two are ends of a modelling choice about how sharply stiffness
falls with equilibrium length, and the theory accommodates either.

\section{Discussion}

The formulation departs from the standard ENM in one respect, coupling the spring
constants to the equilibrium lengths rather than to the equilibrium distances.
The contact map then becomes a property of the parameters, a mutation becomes a
well-defined operation on the network that can create and destroy contacts, and
the mutant structure becomes a derived quantity, obtained by minimising the
mutant potential rather than by responding linearly to an equivalent force.

The two results, eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} and eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian}, show
the same pattern. Each contains a contribution that is always present, plus
contributions that vanish when the reference is unstrained with a fixed contact
map. In the energy these are term (b), proportional to the accumulated strain,
and term (c), arising from changes in stiffness. In the dynamics it is the
transverse contribution $g_{ij}$. A treatment set up at an unstrained wild type
sets all three to zero and is right to do so at that point. Our interest is in
networks reached by mutation, where none of the three is zero and the discarded
terms grow with the quantity that accumulates.

Both results are formal. They establish what the terms are and when they vanish,
not how large they are for any particular protein.

What is not treated here: the substitution process, the statistics of strain
along an evolutionary trajectory, and the selection models that would act on
$\Vmin$. Those are the layers this formulation is meant to support.
