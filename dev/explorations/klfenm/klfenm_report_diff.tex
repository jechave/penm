\maketitle

An elastic network model is specified by the force constants $k_{ij}$ and the
equilibrium lengths $l_{ij}$ of its springs. Here the force constants are taken to
be a function of the equilibrium lengths, $k_{ij} = k(l_{ij})$, which leaves the
$l_{ij}$ as the only free parameters. A mutation perturbs the $l_{ij}$ of one
site, and its consequences are obtained without expanding or linearising the
model. This note derives the potential, its minimum, the conformational
distribution, and the thermodynamic properties, first for a general network and
then for a reference and its mutant, ending with the mutational changes.

\section{The model}

\subsection{Conformations and distances}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{The }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{A }\DIFaddend protein is represented by $N$ nodes\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, one per residue, joined in pairs by springs}\DIFaddend .
A conformation is the set of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{their
positions,
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the node positions,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
\rr = (\rr_1, \rr_2, \ldots, \rr_N) \in \mathbb{R}^{3N}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{,
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{.
}\DIFaddend \end{equation}
\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{We write
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\rr_{ij} \;\equiv\; \rr_j - \rr_i
}\end{equation}
\DIFadd{for the vector from node $i$ to node $j$, so that $\rr_{ij} = -\rr_{ji}$, }\DIFaddend and the
distance between \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{nodes $i$ and $j$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the two nodes }\DIFaddend in conformation $\rr$ is
\begin{equation}
d_{ij}(\rr) \;=\; \lVert \rr\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_i - \rr_j }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_{ij} }\DIFaddend \rVert\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\;=\; }%DIFDELCMD < \big[%%%
\DIFdel{(\rr_i - \rr_j)\cdot(\rr_i - \rr_j)}%DIFDELCMD < \big]%%%
\DIFdel{^{1/2}.
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{,
}\DIFaddend \label{eq:dij}
\end{equation}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{We write $\rr_{ij} = \rr_i - \rr_j$ throughout, so that $\rr_{ij} = -\rr_{ji}$ and
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{which is symmetric, }\DIFaddend $d_{ij} = d_{ji}$.

\subsection{Parameters}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Each }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Every }\DIFaddend pair of nodes \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is joined by a spring, and each spring }\DIFaddend carries an equilibrium
length $l_{ij}$, the length at which \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the
spring joining the pair is unstretched}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{it is neither stretched nor compressed}\DIFaddend . There
is one \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{for every pair, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{such length for every spring, and together they form
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
\LL \;=\; \{\, l_{ij} \,:\, 1 \le i < j \le N \,\},
\qquad
|\LL| = \tfrac12 N(N-1),
\end{equation}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and $\LL$ is }\DIFdelend the complete set of free parameters of the model. The force constant of a \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend is
fixed by its \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{own }\DIFaddend equilibrium length through a function $k$,
\begin{equation}
k_{ij} \;=\; k(l_{ij}),
\qquad k : [0,\infty) \to [0,\infty).
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}

This is the one departure from standard practice\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and it is worth stating
carefully, because in the standard case the departure is invisible}\DIFaddend . A standard
elastic network model is built on a given structure\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, which it takes as the
equilibrium conformation }\DIFaddend $\re$\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and assigns each spring the equilibrium length
$l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\re)$ it already has in that structure. Its force constants are
then written as a function of $d_{ij}(\re)$ --- but since $l_{ij}$ }\DIFaddend and
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{takes $k_{ij} = f(d_{ij}(\re))$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$d_{ij}(\re)$ are equal there}\DIFaddend , a function of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a distance in that structure; here }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{one is a function of the other, and
nothing distinguishes $k(l_{ij})$ from $k(d_{ij}(\re))$. Here the $l_{ij}$ are left
free rather than read off a structure, the two cease to coincide, and
\eqref{eq:kl} states which of them }\DIFaddend $k$ is a function of\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a free parameter of the model.
The function }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{: the parameter $l_{ij}$.
}

\DIFadd{We assume of }\DIFaddend $k$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is otherwise arbitrary. We take it }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{only that it is }\DIFaddend non-increasing, so that \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs with long equilibrium lengths }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{long springs }\DIFaddend are weakly
coupled or uncoupled, and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{we assume
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{that }\DIFaddend $k(l) = 0$ for $l$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{large enough that
distant pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{beyond some range, so that
springs long enough to represent no contact }\DIFaddend do not interact. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Nothing below requires
$k$ to be continuous or differentiable.
}\DIFaddend 

\subsection{The potential}

The potential energy of a conformation $\rr$ for a network with parameters $\LL$
is a sum of harmonic terms, one per \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend ,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
V(\rr; \LL) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l_{ij}\big)^{2}.
\;}
\label{eq:V}
\end{equation}
The sum runs over all $\tfrac12 N(N-1)$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs. Since $V \ge 0$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs, }\DIFaddend and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$V = 0$ only if
every term vanishes, the zero of energy is the fully relaxed configuration, and }\DIFdelend $V$ evaluated at any
conformation is the elastic energy stored in \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the springs. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{them. Every term is non-negative, so
$V \ge 0$, and $V(\rr;\LL) = 0$ requires every spring to sit at its own equilibrium
length, $d_{ij}(\rr) = l_{ij}$ for every active spring at once. Whether any
conformation achieves this is a question about $\LL$, not a property of the model:
for some parameter sets no conformation does, and then $V > 0$ everywhere. Section
\ref{sec:frustration} takes this up.
}\DIFaddend 

A \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend with $k(l_{ij}) > 0$ contributes to $V$ and is said to be \emph{active}; a
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend with $k(l_{ij}) = 0$ contributes nothing. The set of active \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs }\DIFaddend is the
network's contact map. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Because }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Since }\DIFaddend $k$ is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{evaluated at }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a function of }\DIFaddend $l_{ij}$\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and not at
$d_{ij}(\rr)$, which pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, which springs }\DIFaddend are
active is fixed by $\LL$ alone\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{: the contact map is a property of the parameters}\DIFaddend ,
and does not \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{depend
on the conformation at which $V$ is evaluated. }%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdel{Every pair carries a parameter, including the inactive ones. This is necessary if
a mutation is to be able to create a contact: an inactive pair with no $l_{ij}$
could never acquire one inside the range where
$k > 0$, so mutation could only
destroy contacts and never form them}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{change as the network moves. In a standard model, where
$l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\re)$, this same set would be read off the structure instead}\DIFaddend .

\section{The minimum of the potential}

\subsection{The equilibrium condition}

To find the conformations that minimise \eqref{eq:V} we need the gradient of $V$,
and hence the gradient of the distances. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Let
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\ee_{ij} \;\equiv\; \frac{\rr_{ij}}{d_{ij}}
\label{eq:eij}
}\end{equation}
\DIFadd{be the unit vector along the spring, pointing from $i$ to $j$; it satisfies
$\ee_{ij} = -\ee_{ji}$. }\DIFaddend Differentiating $d_{ij}^2 = \rr_{ij}\cdot\rr_{ij}$ with
respect to $\rr_i$, \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and using $\partial \rr_{ij}/\partial\rr_i = -\mathbf{I}$,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
2\, d_{ij} \frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial \rr_i} \;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-\,}\DIFaddend 2\,\rr_{ij},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial \rr_i} \;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-}\DIFaddend \frac{\rr_{ij}}{d_{ij}}
\;\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\equiv}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{=}\DIFaddend \; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-\,}\DIFaddend \ee_{ij}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{,
}%DIFDELCMD < \label{eq:eij}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{.
}\label{eq:ddij}
\DIFaddend \end{equation}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{which defines the unit vector $\ee_{ij}$ along the pair, pointing }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The derivative with respect to $\rr_j$ follows the same way, now with
$\partial \rr_{ij}/\partial\rr_j = +\mathbf{I}$, giving
$\partial d_{ij}/\partial \rr_j = +\ee_{ij}$. Moving node $i$ away from node $j$
lengthens the spring, and \eqref{eq:ddij} records that: the distance decreases
fastest along $-\ee_{ij}$, which points }\DIFaddend from $j$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{to }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{back towards }\DIFaddend $i$.
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Since $d_{ij}$ depends on $\rr_j$ only through $\rr_{ij} = -\rr_{ji}$, we also have
$\partial d_{ij}/\partial \rr_j = -\ee_{ij}$.
}\DIFdelend 

Differentiating \eqref{eq:V} by the chain rule, and noting that $k(l_{ij})$ is a
constant with respect to $\rr$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \rr_i}
\;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-}\DIFaddend \sum_{j \ne i} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l_{ij}\big)\,\ee_{ij}.
\label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
Each term \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is a central force }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{lies }\DIFaddend along the line joining the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{two nodes, so the force it exerts is
central: it has no component transverse to the spring}\DIFaddend . That $k$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{does not
differentiate }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{carries no
derivative }\DIFaddend is a consequence of \eqref{eq:kl}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{: had we taken $k_{ij} =
f(d_{ij}(\rr))$, a term in $f'(d_{ij})$ would
appear here and again in
every
derivative below}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. Had the force constants been written
as a function of the equilibrium distance, $k_{ij} = f(d_{ij}(\re))$, they would
still be constants with respect to $\rr$ and this gradient would be unchanged; what
\eqref{eq:kl} buys is not simplicity here but the freedom, used in
Section~\ref{sec:mutation}, to vary $l_{ij}$ and have $k_{ij}$ follow}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{An }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Given the parameters $\LL$, an }\DIFaddend equilibrium conformation $\re$ is one at which the
gradient vanishes,
\begin{equation}
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \boxed{\;
%DIFDELCMD < \sum_{j \ne i} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\re) - l_{ij}\big)\,\ee^{\,e}_{ij} \;=\; \mathbf{0},
%DIFDELCMD < \qquad i = 1,\ldots,N.
%DIFDELCMD < \;}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \boxed{\;
\sum_{j \ne i} k(l_{ij})\,\big(d_{ij}(\re) - l_{ij}\big)\,\ee^{\,e}_{ij} \;=\; \mathbf{0},
\qquad i = 1,\ldots,N,
\;}
\DIFaddend \label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{where the overall sign of \eqref{eq:grad} has been dropped, the right-hand side
being zero. }\DIFaddend These are $3N$ scalar equations \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for the $3N$ unknown coordinates}\DIFaddend , of
which $3N-6$ are independent: $V$ depends on the conformation only through the
distances \eqref{eq:dij}, so it is invariant under the three translations and three
rotations, and along those six directions the gradient vanishes identically.

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Two consequences }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{One consequence }\DIFaddend of that invariance should be recorded now, because \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{both matter
later. First, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{it matters
later: }\DIFaddend $\re$ is determined only up to a rigid motion.
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Second, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin 

\DIFadd{Separately, and for a different reason, }\DIFaddend $V$ is \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{also }\DIFaddend invariant under reflection\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{as well, since }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{.
This does not follow from invariance under rigid motions --- a reflection is not
one --- but from the same fact as before, that $V$ sees the conformation only
through the distances \eqref{eq:dij}, and }\DIFaddend reflecting a conformation leaves every
$d_{ij}$ unchanged\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{; a }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. A }\DIFaddend chiral $\re$ therefore has a mirror image \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{with the same
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of equal }\DIFaddend energy,
and the minimum of $V$ is not unique. Below, $\re$ denotes one chosen \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{minimiser}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{minimum}\DIFaddend , and
every quantity derived from it is understood relative to that choice.

\subsection{Strain}

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The combination $d_{ij}(\re) - l_{ij}$ appearing in \eqref{eq:equilibrium} is the
amount by which a spring departs from its equilibrium length at the equilibrium
conformation. It is convenient to name it. }\DIFaddend Let $\re$ be an equilibrium
conformation and define, for every \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair,
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
s_{ij} \;\equiv\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{l_{ij} - }\DIFdelend d_{ij}(\re) \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- l_{ij}}\DIFaddend ,
\label{eq:sij}
\end{equation}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the amount by which the spring departs from its equilibrium length there}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{its }\emph{\DIFadd{strain}}\DIFaddend . A spring with $s_{ij} > 0$ is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{compressed}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{longer than its equilibrium length
and so is stretched}\DIFaddend , one with $s_{ij} < 0$ is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{stretched}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{compressed}\DIFaddend , and one with $s_{ij} = 0$
is relaxed.

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Substituting \eqref{eq:sij} into \eqref{eq:V} evaluated at $\re$, and using
$d_{ij}(\re) - l_{ij} = -s_{ij}$, gives the minimum energy
}\begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\boxed{\;
V(\re; \LL) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\, s_{ij}^{2}.
\;}
%DIFDELCMD < \label{eq:Vmin}%%%
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{The }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{In these terms the }\DIFaddend equilibrium condition \eqref{eq:equilibrium} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{may be written in the same terms,
$\sum_{j\ne i} k(l_{ij}) s_{ij} \ee^{\,e}_{ij} = \mathbf{0}$: the }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{reads
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\sum_{j\ne i} k(l_{ij})\, s_{ij}\, \ee^{\,e}_{ij} \;=\; \mathbf{0},
\qquad i = 1,\ldots,N,
\label{eq:equilibrium-s}
}\end{equation}
\DIFadd{so the }\DIFaddend forces on each node cancel, which does not require the individual $s_{ij}$
to vanish. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Substituting \eqref{eq:sij} into \eqref{eq:V} evaluated at $\re$ gives
the minimum energy
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\boxed{\;
V(\re; \LL) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k(l_{ij})\, s_{ij}^{2},
\;}
\label{eq:Vmin}
}\end{equation}
\DIFadd{which depends on the strains only through their squares, and so is unchanged by the
sign convention adopted in \eqref{eq:sij}.
}\DIFaddend 

\subsection{Frustration}
\DIFaddbegin \label{sec:frustration}
\DIFaddend 

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{A network is }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{We call a network }\DIFaddend \textbf{frustrated} when $V(\re;\LL) > 0$\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, that is, when }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{: }\DIFaddend no conformation
relaxes all \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of }\DIFaddend its active springs at once\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and the network sits at a compromise in
which the forces cancel node by node while individual springs remain strained}\DIFaddend . By
\eqref{eq:Vmin} this \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{holds
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is the case }\DIFaddend whenever $s_{ij} \ne 0$ for at least one active
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair. }%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdel{The unfrustrated case is the special solution of \eqref{eq:equilibrium} in which
every term vanishes separately, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring. A network that is not frustrated has }\DIFaddend $s_{ij} = 0$ for \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{all active pairs.
It is
attainable: given any structure , setting $l_{ij} = d_{ij}$ for every pair makes
that structure }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{every active spring,
and then $V(\re;\LL) = 0$.
}

\DIFadd{Whether a given $\LL$ is frustrated is settled by solving \eqref{eq:equilibrium}
for $\re$ and evaluating \eqref{eq:Vmin}. One case can be read off without
solving anything. Choose a structure $\rr^{\ast}$ and set
$l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\rr^{\ast})$ for every spring. Then every strain vanishes at
$\rr^{\ast}$, so \eqref{eq:equilibrium-s} is satisfied term by term,
$\rr^{\ast}$ is }\DIFaddend an equilibrium conformation\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{with $V = 0$, and this is }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and $V(\rr^{\ast};\LL) = 0$: the
network is unfrustrated by construction. This is }\DIFaddend what a standard elastic network
model does\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. But it is not the general solution.
Equation~\eqref{eq:equilibrium}imposes $3N-6$ independent conditions on
$\tfrac12 N(N-1)$ parameters, and for $N$ of any size the system is underdetermined, so sets }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and it is a statement about how }\DIFaddend $\LL$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{with cancelling but individually nonzero $s_{ij}$
are the generic case}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{was chosen, not about
\eqref{eq:equilibrium}, which takes $\LL$ as given.
}

\DIFadd{Nothing obliges $\LL$ to have been chosen that way, and parameter sets not of this
form will in general be frustrated. How the $l_{ij}$ ought to be chosen is a
separate question from the one \eqref{eq:equilibrium} answers, and is not taken up
here; Section~\ref{sec:open} returns to it. What matters below is that both cases
are allowed: nothing in Sections \ref{sec:distribution} to \ref{sec:changes}
assumes an unfrustrated network}\DIFaddend . As will be seen in Section~\ref{sec:mutation}, a
mutation applied to an unfrustrated network produces a frustrated one.

\section{The conformational distribution}
\label{sec:distribution}

\subsection{The Boltzmann distribution}

At temperature $T$ the conformations are distributed according to
\begin{equation}
\rho(\rr) \;=\; \frac{e^{-\beta V(\rr;\LL)}}{Z},
\qquad
Z \;=\; \int e^{-\beta V(\rr;\LL)}\, d\rr,
\qquad
\beta = \frac{1}{k_B T},
\label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
where the integral runs over $\mathbb{R}^{3N}$. This is a configurational
integral: the momenta have been left out. They contribute a factor that depends on
the masses and on $\beta$ but not on $\LL$, so it cancels from every difference
between networks computed in Section~\ref{sec:changes}, which is all we use.

\subsection{The Hessian}

The integral in \eqref{eq:boltzmann} cannot be evaluated in closed form, because
$V$ is not quadratic in $\rr$: the distances \eqref{eq:dij} are nonlinear
functions of the coordinates. We therefore expand $V$ about an equilibrium
conformation. Writing $\mathbf{u} = \rr - \re$,
\begin{equation}
V(\rr;\LL) \;=\; V(\re;\LL)
\;+\; \frac{\partial V}{\partial \rr}\bigg|_{\re}\!\!\cdot \mathbf{u}
\;+\; \tfrac12\, \mathbf{u}^{\mathsf T} \KK\, \mathbf{u} \;+\; O(\mathbf{u}^3),
\qquad
\KK \;=\; \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \rr^2}\bigg|_{\re}.
\label{eq:expansion}
\end{equation}
The linear term vanishes by \eqref{eq:equilibrium}.

To obtain $\KK$ we need the second derivatives of the distances. Differentiating
$\ee_{ij} = \rr_{ij}/d_{ij}$ with respect to $\rr_j$, using the product rule with
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\partial \rr_{ij}/\partial\rr_j = -\mathbf{I}$ and
$\partial d_{ij}^{-1}/\partial \rr_j = -d_{ij}^{-2}(-\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}) =
d_{ij}^{-2}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}$,
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\partial \rr_{ij}/\partial\rr_j = \mathbf{I}$ and
$\partial d_{ij}^{-1}/\partial \rr_j = -d_{ij}^{-2}\,\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}$,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{\partial^2 d_{ij}}{\partial \rr_i \partial \rr_j}
\;=\; }\DIFdelend \frac{\partial \ee_{ij}}{\partial \rr_j}
\;=\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{-}\DIFdelend \frac{\mathbf{I}}{d_{ij}} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{+ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend \frac{\rr_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}}{d_{ij}^{2}}
\;=\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf{I}}{d_{ij}}}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\frac{\mathbf{I} - \ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}}{d_{ij}}}\DIFaddend .
\label{eq:d2d}
\end{equation}
Now differentiate the gradient \eqref{eq:grad} with respect to $\rr_j$ for $j \ne i$.
Only the single term of the sum with that index depends on $\rr_j$, and it is a
product of $(d_{ij} - l_{ij})$ and $\ee_{ij}$, so
\begin{equation}
\KK_{ij}
\;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-\,}\DIFaddend k(l_{ij})\left[
\frac{\partial\, (d_{ij} - l_{ij})}{\partial \rr_j}\,\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}
\;+\; (d_{ij} - l_{ij})\, \frac{\partial \ee_{ij}}{\partial \rr_j}
\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\partial d_{ij}/\partial\rr_j = -\ee_{ij}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\partial d_{ij}/\partial\rr_j = \ee_{ij}$ }\DIFaddend and \eqref{eq:d2d}, and
then \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$d_{ij} - l_{ij} = -s_{ij}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$d_{ij} - l_{ij} = s_{ij}$ }\DIFaddend from \eqref{eq:sij},
\begin{equation}
\KK_{ij}
\;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-\,}\DIFaddend k(l_{ij})\left[ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{-}\DIFdelend \ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{- }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{+ }\DIFaddend s_{ij}\,\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf{I}}{d_{ij}} }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\frac{\mathbf{I} - \ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}}{d_{ij}} }\DIFaddend \right],
\end{equation}
that is, defining $g_{ij} \equiv s_{ij}/d_{ij}(\re)$,
\begin{equation}
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \boxed{\;
%DIFDELCMD < \KK_{ij} \;=\; -\,k(l_{ij})\Big[\,\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}
%DIFDELCMD < + g_{ij}\big(\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf{I}\big)\Big],
%DIFDELCMD < \qquad i \ne j.
%DIFDELCMD < \;}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \boxed{\;
\KK_{ij} \;=\; -\,k(l_{ij})\Big[\,\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}
- g_{ij}\big(\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf{I}\big)\Big],
\qquad i \ne j.
\;}
\DIFaddend \label{eq:hessian}
\end{equation}
The diagonal blocks follow from translational invariance. Displacing every node by
the same vector $\mathbf{a}$ leaves $V$ unchanged, so $\KK$ annihilates that
displacement, and summing the blocks in a row gives
$\sum_{j} \KK_{ij}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$ for every $\mathbf{a}$, whence
\begin{equation}
\KK_{ii} \;=\; -\sum_{j \ne i} \KK_{ij}.
\label{eq:Kii}
\end{equation}
Inactive \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs }\DIFaddend have $k(l_{ij}) = 0$ and contribute nothing, so $\KK$ is
$3N \times 3N$ however many \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs }\DIFaddend carry parameters.

\subsection{Longitudinal and transverse stiffness}

Equation \eqref{eq:hessian} contains two projectors. Writing
$\mathbf{P}_\perp = \mathbf{I} - \ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}$ for the projector
onto the plane perpendicular to the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend , it may be rearranged as
\begin{equation}
\KK_{ij} \;=\; -\,k(l_{ij})\Big[\ee_{ij}\ee_{ij}^{\mathsf T}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{- }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{+ }\DIFaddend g_{ij}\,\mathbf{P}_\perp\Big],
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
so a \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend carries two stiffnesses rather than one: a longitudinal $k(l_{ij})$
resisting change of the distance, and a transverse \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$-k(l_{ij})g_{ij}$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$k(l_{ij})g_{ij}$}\DIFaddend , doubly
degenerate, resisting rotation of the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend at fixed distance.

The transverse stiffness is proportional to \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$g_{ij} = s_{ij}/d_{ij}$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$g_{ij} = s_{ij}/d_{ij}(\re)$}\DIFaddend , and so
vanishes for a relaxed spring and changes sign with the strain. A stretched spring
(\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$s_{ij}<0$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$s_{ij}>0$}\DIFaddend ) resists transverse displacement, as a taut string does. A compressed
one (\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$s_{ij}>0$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$s_{ij}<0$}\DIFaddend ) has negative transverse stiffness: displacing the two nodes
sideways increases their separation, which a compressed spring favours. Note that
it is the transverse stiffness that is negative, not $k(l_{ij})$, which is
non-negative by \eqref{eq:kl}.

Since $g_{ij} = 0$ for every \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend of an unfrustrated network, the transverse term
is absent there, and \eqref{eq:hessian} reduces to the rank-one form of a standard
elastic network model. Frustration enters the conformational distribution through
$g_{ij}$ and through nothing else.

\subsection{The Gaussian approximation}

Truncating \eqref{eq:expansion} after the quadratic term gives
\begin{equation}
V(\rr;\LL) \;\simeq\; V(\re;\LL) + \tfrac12 \mathbf{u}^{\mathsf T}\KK\mathbf{u},
\label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}
and hence, from \eqref{eq:boltzmann}, a Gaussian distribution of conformations
centred on $\re$,
\begin{equation}
\rho(\rr) \;\simeq\; \frac{1}{Z}\, e^{-\beta V(\re;\LL)}\,
\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\beta}{2}\,\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf T}\KK\,\mathbf{u}\big).
\label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}

$\KK$ is symmetric, and positive semi-definite because $\re$ is a minimum. Let its
eigenvalues be $\lambda_n$ with eigenvectors $\mathbf{q}_n$. At least six
eigenvalues vanish, corresponding to the three translations and three rotations
along which $V$ does not vary.

Whether there are exactly six is a property of the network, not a given. A
displacement $\mathbf{u}$ is a null direction of $\KK$ when it changes no active
distance to first order, that is when $\ee_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{u}_j) = 0$
for every active \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend . A network for which the only such displacements are the six
rigid motions is \emph{infinitesimally rigid}, and then $\mathrm{rank}\,\KK = 3N-6$.
Connectivity does not suffice: a node joined to the rest by a single active spring
can swing about it, and one joined by two can rotate about the axis through them,
in both cases without changing any active distance. Each such freedom is a further
null direction. In what follows the networks are assumed infinitesimally rigid,
and we write $\sum'$ and $\prod'$ for sums and products over the $3N-6$ nonzero
eigenvalues.

The distribution \eqref{eq:gaussian} is flat along the six null directions, which
is the rigid-body freedom of the whole network, and we factor it out: displacements
are taken in the complement, where the covariance is
\begin{equation}
\bm{\Sigma} \;=\; \beta^{-1}\KK^{+},
\qquad
\KK^{+} \;=\; \sum_n{}' \lambda_n^{-1}\, \mathbf{q}_n \mathbf{q}_n^{\mathsf T},
\label{eq:covariance}
\end{equation}
with $\KK^{+}$ the pseudo-inverse obtained by inverting the nonzero eigenvalues
and annihilating the null directions.

\section{Thermodynamic properties}
\label{sec:thermo}

\subsection{The partition function}

In the quadratic approximation \eqref{eq:quadratic} the integral
\eqref{eq:boltzmann} factorises in the eigenbasis of $\KK$. Writing
$\mathbf{u} = \sum_n y_n \mathbf{q}_n$, the quadratic form is
$\sum_n \lambda_n y_n^2$, and the integral over each of the $3N-6$ non-null
directions is a standard Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\beta\lambda_n y_n^2/2}\, dy_n
\;=\; \left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta\lambda_n}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
Z \;=\; e^{-\beta V(\re;\LL)} \prod_n{}' \left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta\lambda_n}\right)^{1/2},
\qquad
\ln Z \;=\; -\beta V(\re;\LL) + \tfrac12 \sum_n{}' \ln\frac{2\pi}{\beta\lambda_n}.
\;}
\label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Free energy, internal energy, entropy}

The Helmholtz free energy is
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; -\beta^{-1}\ln Z
\;=\; V(\re;\LL) \;-\; \frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_n{}' \ln\frac{2\pi}{\beta\lambda_n}.
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
The internal energy follows by differentiating \eqref{eq:Z}, using
$\partial\ln\lambda_n/\partial\beta = 0$,
\begin{equation}
U \;=\; -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}
\;=\; V(\re;\LL) \;+\; \frac{3N-6}{2\beta},
\label{eq:U}
\end{equation}
which is the minimum energy plus $\tfrac12 k_B T$ per non-rigid degree of freedom,
as equipartition requires. The entropy is $S = (U-A)/T$, so
\begin{equation}
T S \;=\; U - A \;=\; \frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_n{}'
\left[\ln\frac{2\pi}{\beta\lambda_n} + 1\right].
\label{eq:S}
\end{equation}
Soft directions, those of small $\lambda_n$, contribute most: the network gains
entropy by being easy to deform.

Two comments on \eqref{eq:A} and \eqref{eq:S}. The logarithm takes a dimensional
argument, so the numerical values of $A$ and $TS$ depend on the units chosen for
length, and $A$ may fall below $V(\re;\LL)$. Neither is meaningful alone. What is
meaningful is a difference between two networks at the same temperature, where the
units cancel; those are the quantities of Section~\ref{sec:changes}. The same
applies to the momentum factor dropped in \eqref{eq:boltzmann}, and to the
rigid-body volume factored out of \eqref{eq:covariance}: both are common to the
two networks and cancel.

\jc{Open question. Equations \eqref{eq:A} and \eqref{eq:S} rest on the quadratic
truncation \eqref{eq:quadratic}. How good it is for a frustrated network, where the
transverse term makes $\KK$ differ from the standard form, has not been examined
here.}

\section{Reference and mutant}
\label{sec:mutation}

\subsection{The reference network}

Everything above applies to any network. We now fix one, the \emph{reference},
with parameters $\LL^{\text{ref}} = \{l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\}$ and potential
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{ref}}(\rr) \;\equiv\; V(\rr; \LL^{\text{ref}})
\;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k\big(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\big)
\Big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\Big)^{2}.
\label{eq:Vref}
\end{equation}
Write $k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} = k(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij})$ for its force constants,
$\re_{\text{ref}}$ for an equilibrium conformation obtained from
\eqref{eq:equilibrium}, and
\begin{equation}
s_{ij} \;=\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} - }\DIFdelend d_{ij}(\re_{\text{ref}}) \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}
}\DIFaddend \label{eq:sref}
\end{equation}
for its strains. Its Hessian $\KK^{\text{ref}}$, spectrum
$\{\lambda^{\text{ref}}_n\}$, and thermodynamic properties $A_{\text{ref}}$,
$U_{\text{ref}}$, $S_{\text{ref}}$ follow from Sections \ref{sec:distribution} and
\ref{sec:thermo}. The reference may be frustrated or not; nothing below assumes
either.

\subsection{Mutation}

A mutation at site $m$ perturbs the equilibrium lengths of the springs that
connect $m$ to the rest of the network, and leaves all others unchanged:
\begin{equation}
l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} \;=\; l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij},
\qquad
\delta l_{ij} = 0 \ \text{ unless } i = m \text{ or } j = m.
\label{eq:mutation}
\end{equation}
The perturbations $\delta l_{ij}$ are drawn from a distribution with zero mean and
variance $\sigma^2$, independently for each affected \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend ; $\sigma$ sets the size
of the mutation.

The mutant potential is \eqref{eq:V} evaluated with these parameters,
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{mut}}(\rr) \;\equiv\; V(\rr; \LL^{\text{mut}})
\;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k\big(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}\big)
\Big(d_{ij}(\rr) - l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} - \delta l_{ij}\Big)^{2}.
\label{eq:Vmut}
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:Vref} and \eqref{eq:Vmut}: the mutation changes both the
equilibrium lengths and, through \eqref{eq:kl}, the force constants. Define
\begin{equation}
\delta k_{ij} \;\equiv\; k\big(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}\big)
- k\big(l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\big),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
k^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta k_{ij}.
\label{eq:dk}
\end{equation}
This is an exact difference of two values of $k$, not a differential: no
assumption is made that $\delta l_{ij}$ is small or that $k$ is differentiable.
A \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend whose equilibrium length crosses from a region where $k>0$ to one where
$k=0$, or the reverse, has $\delta k_{ij}$ of the size of $k$ itself. Contacts are
created and destroyed here, in the parameters, and not by any change in
conformation.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{This is why every spring carries an equilibrium length, the inactive ones
included. An inactive spring with no $l_{ij}$ could never acquire one inside the
range where $k > 0$, so a mutation could only destroy contacts and never form
them; carrying the parameter regardless lets \eqref{eq:mutation} move a spring in
either direction across that range.
}

\DIFaddend The mutant has its own equilibrium conformation $\re_{\text{mut}}$, obtained from
\eqref{eq:equilibrium} with the mutant parameters, its own strains
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$s^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} - d_{ij}(\re_{\text{mut}})$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$s^{\text{mut}}_{ij} = d_{ij}(\re_{\text{mut}}) - l^{\text{mut}}_{ij}$}\DIFaddend , its own
Hessian $\KK^{\text{mut}}$ and spectrum $\{\lambda^{\text{mut}}_n\}$, and its own
$A_{\text{mut}}$, $U_{\text{mut}}$, $S_{\text{mut}}$. Note that $\re_{\text{mut}}$
is obtained by minimising \eqref{eq:Vmut} itself, not by displacing
$\re_{\text{ref}}$ along a response to the perturbation.

Even if the reference is unfrustrated, the mutant will in general be frustrated:
the perturbed lengths $l^{\text{mut}}_{ij}$ need not be simultaneously realisable
by any conformation, so the mutant settles at a compromise with $s^{\text{mut}}_{ij}
\ne 0$.

\section{Mutational changes}
\label{sec:changes}

\subsection{Three energy differences}

Two networks and two conformations give three energy differences worth naming. The
first is the cost of changing the parameters at fixed conformation,
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\text{stress}} \;=\;
V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{ref}}) - V_{\text{ref}}(\re_{\text{ref}}),
\label{eq:dvstress}
\end{equation}
the second the energy released as the mutant relaxes to its own equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\text{relax}} \;=\;
V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{mut}}) - V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{ref}}),
\label{eq:dvrelax}
\end{equation}
and their sum is the difference between the two minima,
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\min} \;=\;
V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{mut}}) - V_{\text{ref}}(\re_{\text{ref}})
\;=\; \Delta V_{\text{stress}} + \Delta V_{\text{relax}}.
\label{eq:dvmin}
\end{equation}
Since $\re_{\text{mut}}$ minimises $V_{\text{mut}}$, we have
$V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{mut}}) \le V_{\text{mut}}(\rr)$ for every $\rr$, and in
particular for $\rr = \re_{\text{ref}}$, so
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\text{relax}} \;\le\; 0 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decomposition of the stress energy}
\label{sec:decomposition}

$\Delta V_{\text{stress}}$ can be written out in closed form. We do so in steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: separate the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs}\DIFaddend .}
Both \eqref{eq:Vref} and \eqref{eq:Vmut} are sums over the same index set, all
$\tfrac12 N(N-1)$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs}\DIFaddend , so their difference is a sum of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair terms}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{one term per spring}\DIFaddend ,
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\text{stress}} \;=\; \sum_{i<j} \left[
\tfrac12 k^{\text{mut}}_{ij}\big(d^e_{ij} - l^{\text{mut}}_{ij}\big)^2
- \tfrac12 k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\big(d^e_{ij} - l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\big)^2
\right],
\qquad d^e_{ij} \equiv d_{ij}(\re_{\text{ref}}).
\end{equation}
This is where carrying a parameter for every \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend , active or not, does its work:
no \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend appears in one sum and not the other, and a contact created or destroyed
by the mutation is carried by $\delta k_{ij}$ rather than by a change in the range
of summation.

\paragraph{Step 2: eliminate the distance.}
By the definition of the reference strain \eqref{eq:sref},
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$d^e_{ij} = l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} - s_{ij}$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$d^e_{ij} = l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + s_{ij}$}\DIFaddend , so the two brackets become
\begin{equation}
d^e_{ij} - l^{\text{ref}}_{ij} \;=\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{-\,}\DIFdelend s_{ij},
\qquad
d^e_{ij} - l^{\text{mut}}_{ij} \;=\; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{-\,}\DIFdelend s_{ij} - \delta l_{ij}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\;=\; -}%DIFDELCMD < \big(%%%
\DIFdel{s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}}%DIFDELCMD < \big)%%%
\DIFdelend .
\end{equation}
The conformation has now disappeared from the problem: what remains is expressed
entirely in the reference strains and the perturbation.

\paragraph{Step 3: substitute the force constants.}
Using \eqref{eq:dk}, the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair term is
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{term for one spring is
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
\tfrac12 \big(k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} + \delta k_{ij}\big)
\big(s_{ij} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{+ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend \delta l_{ij}\big)^2
- \tfrac12 k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, s_{ij}^2 .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 4: split off the change in stiffness.}
Grouping the two terms in $k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}$ and keeping the $\delta k_{ij}$
term apart,
\begin{equation}
\;=\; \tfrac12 k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\Big[\big(s_{ij} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{+ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend \delta l_{ij}\big)^2 - s_{ij}^2\Big]
\;+\; \tfrac12\, \delta k_{ij} \big(s_{ij} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{+ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend \delta l_{ij}\big)^2 .
\end{equation}
The second piece is already in final form. No expansion has been made in obtaining
it, and none is possible: $\delta k_{ij}$ is not assumed small.

\paragraph{Step 5: expand the square.}
The bracket is a difference of two squares,
\begin{equation}
\big(s_{ij} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{+ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend \delta l_{ij}\big)^2 - s_{ij}^2
\;=\; \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-\,}\DIFaddend 2\, s_{ij}\, \delta l_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}^2 ,
\end{equation}
which is a finite algebraic identity, not a truncated expansion: it is exact for
any $\delta l_{ij}$.

\paragraph{Step 6: collect.}
Summing over \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs,
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \boxed{\;
%DIFDELCMD < \Delta V_{\text{stress}} \;=\;
%DIFDELCMD < \underbrace{\tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, \delta l_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{(a)}}
%DIFDELCMD < \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, s_{ij}\, \delta l_{ij}}_{\text{(b)}}
%DIFDELCMD < \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} \delta k_{ij}
%DIFDELCMD < \big(s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij}\big)^{2}}_{\text{(c)}} .
%DIFDELCMD < \;}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \boxed{\;
\Delta V_{\text{stress}} \;=\;
\underbrace{\tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, \delta l_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{(a)}}
\;-\; \underbrace{\sum_{i<j} k^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\, s_{ij}\, \delta l_{ij}}_{\text{(b)}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 \sum_{i<j} \delta k_{ij}
\big(s_{ij} - \delta l_{ij}\big)^{2}}_{\text{(c)}} .
\;}
\DIFaddend \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
This holds for any $k$, any mutation size, and any reference network, frustrated or
not.

\subsection{The three terms}

Term (a) comes from the $\delta l_{ij}^2$ of the expanded square. It is
non-negative, quadratic in the size of the mutation, and independent of the state
of the reference.

Term (b) is the cross term \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$2 s_{ij}\delta l_{ij}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$-2 s_{ij}\delta l_{ij}$ }\DIFaddend of the same square. It is
proportional to the reference strain and is the only term of the three that can be
negative\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{: }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. It contributes $-k^{\text{ref}}_{ij} s_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij}$ per spring,
which is negative when $s_{ij}$ and $\delta l_{ij}$ share a sign: lengthening the
equilibrium length of a stretched spring ($s_{ij} > 0$, $\delta l_{ij} > 0$), or
shortening that of }\DIFaddend a \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{mutation that shortens a stretched spring , or lengthens a }\DIFdelend compressed one, \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{moves the equilibrium length towards the
distance the spring actually spans and so }\DIFaddend releases energy. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{It }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The opposite pairing
costs energy. Term (b) }\DIFaddend vanishes when $s_{ij}\,\delta l_{ij} = 0$ for every \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring}\DIFaddend ,
which is to say when the reference is unstrained on the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pairs }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{springs }\DIFaddend the mutation
touches. In particular it vanishes for any mutation of an unfrustrated reference,
and it does not vanish in general.

Term (c) is the whole of the contribution from the change in force constants. It
vanishes when \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\delta k_{ij}(s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij})^2 = 0$ pairwise}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\delta k_{ij}(s_{ij} - \delta l_{ij})^2 = 0$ for every spring}\DIFaddend , in
particular when $k$ is constant over the range the mutation explores. It is the
term that a model with a fixed contact map does not have.

Terms (b) and (c) are what distinguish \eqref{eq:decomposition} from the energy
cost of mutating an unfrustrated network with fixed force constants, which is (a)
alone. Neither is a correction of higher order in the mutation size: both are
exact contributions of the same standing as (a), which happen to vanish in that
case.

\subsection{Differences and energies}

$\Delta V_{\text{stress}}$ is a difference of two potentials, and is not the same
as the absolute energy $V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{ref}})$ of the mutant network at
the reference conformation. By \eqref{eq:dvstress} the two differ by
$V_{\text{ref}}(\re_{\text{ref}})$, the strain energy the reference already
carries, which by \eqref{eq:Vmin} is zero for an unfrustrated reference and
positive otherwise.

\subsection{Changes in the thermodynamic properties}

Applying Section~\ref{sec:thermo} to both networks and subtracting, the internal
energy difference follows from \eqref{eq:U},
\begin{equation}
\Delta U \;=\; U_{\text{mut}} - U_{\text{ref}}
\;=\; V_{\text{mut}}(\re_{\text{mut}}) - V_{\text{ref}}(\re_{\text{ref}})
\;=\; \Delta V_{\min},
\end{equation}
the equipartition terms cancelling. This step, and the entropy difference below,
require the two networks to have the same number of non-null directions. A point
mutation changes the parameters of one site and neither adds nor removes a node,
so both Hessians are $3N\times3N$; and if both networks are infinitesimally rigid
in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:distribution}, both have $3N-6$ nonzero
eigenvalues. For the entropy, write \eqref{eq:S} as
\begin{equation}
TS \;=\; \frac{1}{2\beta}\left[ (3N-6)\left(\ln\frac{2\pi}{\beta} + 1\right)
- \sum_n{}' \ln \lambda_n \right],
\end{equation}
in which only the last term depends on the network. Both networks have the same
number of non-null directions, so on subtracting, the constant term cancels and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
T \Delta S \;=\; \frac{1}{2\beta}\left[
\sum_n{}' \ln \lambda^{\text{ref}}_n - \sum_n{}' \ln \lambda^{\text{mut}}_n \right]
\;=\; \frac{1}{2\beta} \ln
\frac{\prod_n' \lambda^{\text{ref}}_n}{\prod_n' \lambda^{\text{mut}}_n} .
\;}
\label{eq:dS}
\end{equation}
The two spectra enter only through these products. No correspondence between the
modes of the reference and those of the mutant is used, and none is available:
the eigenvectors of $\KK^{\text{ref}}$ and $\KK^{\text{mut}}$ are different sets of
directions, and the eigenvalues are not paired by any property of the model.

The free energy difference is then
\begin{equation}
\Delta A \;=\; \Delta U - T\Delta S
\;=\; \Delta V_{\min} - \frac{1}{2\beta} \ln
\frac{\prod_n' \lambda^{\text{ref}}_n}{\prod_n' \lambda^{\text{mut}}_n}.
\label{eq:dA}
\end{equation}
The dimensional ambiguity noted after \eqref{eq:S} has cancelled: \eqref{eq:dS} and
\eqref{eq:dA} depend only on the ratio of the two products. In the protein
literature the quantity \eqref{eq:dA} is what is meant by $\Delta\Delta G$ for the
folded state.

The products $\prod_n' \lambda_n$ are the pseudo-determinants of the two Hessians,
so \eqref{eq:dS} may be computed without diagonalising either matrix.

Equations \eqref{eq:dvmin} and \eqref{eq:dS} separate the two ways a mutation
changes the free energy. It shifts the minimum of the potential, which
\eqref{eq:decomposition} decomposes; and it changes the spectrum of $\KK$, which
by \eqref{eq:hessian} depends on the strains through $g_{ij}$ and on the contact
map through $k(l_{ij})$. A mutation that leaves the minimum energy untouched can
still change the free energy, through the spectrum alone.

\section{The step function}

The results above hold for any $k$. The common choice is a step at a cutoff
distance,
\begin{equation}
k(l) \;=\; k_0\, \mathds{1}\big[\, l \le d_{\max} \,\big],
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
under which a \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair is either a spring of }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring is either active with }\DIFaddend strength $k_0$ or \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{nothing at all}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{inactive}\DIFaddend . Two
consequences follow from the results above.

First, $V$ is discontinuous in the parameters. If a mutation carries
$l^{\text{ref}}_{ij}$ across $d_{\max}$ then $\delta k_{ij} = \pm k_0$, and by term
(c) of \eqref{eq:decomposition} the energy changes by
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\pm\tfrac12 k_0 (s_{ij} + \delta l_{ij})^2$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\pm\tfrac12 k_0 (s_{ij} - \delta l_{ij})^2$ }\DIFaddend however small $\delta l_{ij}$ is.

Second, and for the same reason, term (c) cannot be treated as small when
$\delta l_{ij}$ is small. This is the sense in which the contact map resists a
perturbative treatment: $\delta k_{ij}$ is not controlled by $\delta l_{ij}$.

A continuous $k$, for instance a sigmoid of width $w$ centred on $d_{\max}$, makes
$V$ continuous in $\LL$ and $\delta k_{ij}$ small whenever $\delta l_{ij}$ is
small. The cost is that no \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{pair }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{spring }\DIFaddend is exactly inactive, so the contact map becomes a
threshold imposed on a continuum rather than a property of the network.

\section{Open questions and work to come}
\DIFaddbegin \label{sec:open}
\DIFaddend 

The following are not settled here.

\begin{itemize}
\item The quadratic truncation \eqref{eq:quadratic} is the only approximation in
the document. Its accuracy for a frustrated network has not been examined.
\item The magnitudes of (a), (b) and (c) in \eqref{eq:decomposition} for a real
protein are not known. The decomposition says when each vanishes, not how large
each is.
\item How large \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$s_{ij}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$|s_{ij}|$ }\DIFaddend may become before a compressed spring \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{($s_{ij} < 0$)}\DIFaddend ,
with its negative transverse stiffness \eqref{eq:split}, ceases to represent a
residue contact.
\item A mutation can cost the network its infinitesimal rigidity. The perturbation
\eqref{eq:mutation} acts on every spring of the mutated site, and if enough of
their equilibrium lengths leave the range where $k>0$, the site is left attached
by one or two springs and acquires a rotational freedom that changes no active
distance. The mutant Hessian then has more than six null directions, the counts
in Section~\ref{sec:changes} no longer match, and neither $\Delta U =
\Delta V_{\min}$ nor \eqref{eq:dS} holds as written: an uncancelled term in
$\ln(2\pi/\beta)$ survives, so $\Delta A$ is no longer independent of the units of
length. How often this happens at a given mutation size and cutoff is an empirical
question, and what the free energy difference should mean when it does is not
settled here.
\item \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Whether a }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{How the parameters $\LL$ should be chosen. Section~\ref{sec:frustration}
gives one recipe, $l_{ij} = d_{ij}(\rr^{\ast})$ for a chosen structure, which
yields an unfrustrated network; but nothing selects it, and the question of
whether a }\DIFaddend frustrated network is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend better model of a native structure \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{than an
unfrustrated one, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{--- }\DIFaddend and if
so\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{how the $l_{ij}$ should be chosen, given that
\eqref{eq:equilibrium} does not
determine them}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, which one --- is open. This is a question about the parameters, and is not
one \eqref{eq:equilibrium} addresses: that equation takes $\LL$ as given and
solves for the conformation}\DIFaddend .
\end{itemize}
